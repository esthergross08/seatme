# SeatMe Instagram Launch Campaign

Drafted 2026-08-18. A starting plan for the new @SeatMe-ish account — positioning, content pillars, a concrete first-month calendar, and how to grow it without it feeling like an ad account. Treat this as a first draft to react to, not a final plan.

## The goal, stated plainly

Memes don't sell software directly — people don't screenshot a funny post and go create an account mid-laugh. What they do is follow, share to the group chat, and tag the friend who's mid-meltdown over their own seating chart. The job of this account for the first few months is reach and recall, not conversion: get "SeatMe" associated with "the seating chart people who get it," so that when someone's actually deep in wedding planning and searching for a tool, the name is already familiar instead of a cold discovery. Direct signups from the bio link are a bonus, not the primary metric, especially in month one.

## Audience and voice

Primary audience is engaged couples in the thick of planning (roughly 6-2 months out, when seating actually becomes real), but the funniest and most shareable content usually lands better with the people *around* the couple — maids of honor, wedding planners, and anyone who's ever been on the giving or receiving end of seating-chart politics. Those people share more (they're not self-conscious about it being "their" wedding) and many of them plan events themselves eventually.

Voice: the exhausted, deadpan friend who's been through it — not a brand account cheerfully selling software, not mean-spirited about anyone specific. The research pulled up one genuinely good framing worth stealing as a recurring bit: *"non-planners think a seating chart is cute. planners know it's advanced geometry."* That gap — outsider naivety vs. insider trauma — is a well that can be drawn from repeatedly.

Guardrail: humor should target the *situation* (feuding relatives, the ex who "has to be invited," the toddler cousin chaos, the plus-one nobody's met) — not real identifiable people, and nothing that reads as mocking guests with disabilities, dietary needs, or family structures. Keep the target squarely "seating charts are hard," never "these specific kinds of people are annoying."

## Content pillars

**1. Relatable pain (the bulk of the account).** The universal seating-chart stress points: the divorced-parents-who-can't-be-near-each-other table, the "who sits with the weird uncle" dilemma, the couple friend group breakup that happened three weeks before the wedding, the kids' table chaos, the "we invited 150 people and have 140 seats" math problem. These don't need to mention SeatMe at all most of the time — they just need to be funny and true.

**2. "POV" / relationship-to-planning bits.** Short Reels from a specific point of view: the maid of honor watching the bride redo the chart for the fifth time; the dad who just wants to know where he's sitting; the couple three glasses of wine into "just moving Aunt Carol one more time."

**3. Format-driven memes.** Recurring visual templates that are easy to batch and instantly recognizable once the account has posted a few: a "seating chart difficulty tier list" (round table vs. long table vs. "we have 12 kids and no idea what to do with them"), a two-panel "expectation vs. reality" of the seating chart, a "red flags this table is going to be a disaster" carousel.

**4. Soft product tie-ins (used sparingly — roughly 1 in 6-8 posts).** The same relatable pain points, but landing on "...which is exactly why we built the auto-generate button" or "...so we made a 'these two cannot sit together' rule you can just click." These work best when the joke stands on its own even to someone who's never heard of SeatMe, with the product mention as the punchline's resolution rather than the joke itself — nobody shares an ad.

**5. Community/UGC bait.** Prompts designed to generate comments and shares rather than just views: "tag the friend who's currently mid seating-chart-breakdown," "drop your worst seating chart story," "rate this seating chart disaster 1-10." These drive comments and shares specifically, which is what the algorithm currently weights most heavily (see below).

## Format mix and cadence

Current Instagram guidance (checked 2026-08-18) is fairly specific and worth following rather than guessing: Reels get meaningfully more reach than static posts or carousels for a new account, and the platform's own team has said watch time, likes-per-reach, and **DM shares/sends** are the three biggest ranking signals right now — carousels convert better with an existing audience but Reels are what get an account in front of new people, which matters most for a launch. A reasonable weekly mix to start: 3-4 Reels, 2-3 carousels, 1-2 static posts. One important guardrail from the research: Instagram is now actively penalizing recycled/watermarked content pulled from TikTok via an "Originality Score" — so content needs to be shot or built natively for this account, not reposted wedding-meme content from elsewhere, even with light edits.

## First-month launch calendar

A starting skeleton — 4 weeks, roughly 5-6 posts/week mixing the pillars above. Treat the specific gags as seeds, not a shot list; the point is the shape (mostly pure relatable humor, one soft tie-in a week, one UGC-bait post a week).

**Week 1 — introduce the voice, no hard product pitch yet.**
An intro Reel establishing the account's whole premise: "things only people who've made a wedding seating chart understand," fast cuts of chaotic sticky-note/whiteboard seating attempts. A carousel: "seating chart difficulty tiers" from "10 friends at a backyard BBQ" to "300 guests, 4 divorces between the families, 2 feuding sets of grandparents." A POV Reel: the maid of honor's face when the bride says "ok WAIT, what if we move the whole thing around again." UGC post: "tag the friend currently losing it over their seating chart." Close the week with the "non-planners think it's cute, planners know it's advanced geometry" post — this is a strong, proven angle, good for week one.

**Week 2 — go deeper on the specific dramas.**
The divorced-parents-table dilemma as a Reel. A carousel of "red flags your seating chart is about to be a disaster" (two exes at one table, the group chat that stopped talking in March, three generations of the same argument about religion). A "rating seating chart horror stories" format using submitted/imagined stories to invite comments. First soft product tie-in of the campaign: a Reel building up the "must NOT sit together" problem as pure chaos/comedy, landing on "...so yeah, we built a button for this" with a 2-second screen-recording of the constraint feature — kept short, kept funny first.

**Week 3 — the numbers/logistics jokes.**
"When you have 150 guests and 140 seats" panic Reel. A "kids table chaos" carousel (the toddler who won't sit still, the 9-year-old who declared themselves "too old" for the kids table, the baby that needs a high chair wedged in somewhere). A two-panel expectation-vs-reality post (Pinterest-board seating chart vs. what actually happens at 11pm the night before). Second soft tie-in: the "150 guests, 140 seats" panic Reel resolving with the auto-generate feature actually solving the math in seconds.

**Week 4 — lean into shareability and start measuring what's working.**
Revisit whichever format from weeks 1-3 got the most saves/shares and do a sequel/variation — audiences reward recognizable recurring bits. A community post explicitly asking followers what seating-chart chaos they want to see covered next (this doubles as free content research). Close the month with a slightly more direct but still light post: a short "behind the account" Reel introducing that this is from the team building SeatMe, without ever having led with that.

## Growth tactics beyond the posting schedule

Hashtags: mix broad wedding-planning tags (#weddingplanning, #engaged, #bridetobe) with the more specific, less crowded ones the research surfaced as already active for this exact content, like #familydrama used in seating-chart contexts — less competition, more targeted audience. Engage don't just post: commenting genuinely (not spammily) on established wedding-meme and wedding-planning accounts' posts, especially ones with active comment sections, is a legitimate way to get discovered before the account has its own following. Consider reaching out to one or two existing wedding-meme or wedding-planner-creator accounts for a collab or duet once there's a small library of content to point to — borrowed audience is the fastest way to get real traction on a brand-new account. Bio link should go to a dedicated, trackable landing page (even just a UTM-tagged link to the homepage) so it's possible to see whether Instagram traffic is actually converting once there's enough volume to measure.

## What to track

Follower count matters less than: saves and shares per post (the actual "this is good enough to send to someone" signal), comments (community-building signal and the algorithm's second-most weighted factor), and — once there's meaningful traffic — click-throughs on the bio link and whether any of that traffic actually signs up. Worth a monthly check-in rather than obsessing daily; meme accounts are genuinely a slow build, and the first month is mostly about finding which specific jokes actually land with this specific audience before doubling down.
